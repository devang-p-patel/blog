The tools we use to think shape the thoughts we have.

This isn't a new observation. Marshall McLuhan spent a career on variations of it. But it's one of those ideas that requires re-discovering personally, because the effect is invisible when you're inside it.

I've been noticing this more lately as I've switched between different writing and thinking environments. The same problem, approached in a plain text file versus a collaborative doc versus a structured note-taking app, yields meaningfully different results — not just different formats, but different ideas.

## Tools Encode Assumptions

Every tool is a theory about how work should be done.

A spreadsheet embeds assumptions about data: that information comes in rows and columns, that relationships are numerical, that the important thing about a piece of data is its relationship to other pieces of data. These assumptions are so deeply embedded in the tool that using a spreadsheet tends to make everything look like tabular data.

A word processor embeds assumptions about writing: that the document is the product, that the structure of the document matters, that writing moves roughly from blank page to finished thing. Long-form thinkers have been fighting against this model for decades — building tools (outliners, non-linear writing environments, networked note systems) that embed different assumptions.

The most interesting design question isn't "what should this tool do?" It's "what assumptions is this tool making, and are those the right ones for this kind of work?"

## The Visible and the Invisible

What makes this hard is that the assumptions of our tools are mostly invisible to us.

When you use a tool daily, its affordances become extensions of your thinking. The friction disappears. You stop noticing what the tool makes easy (and therefore likely) versus what it makes hard (and therefore unlikely).

This is good for productivity. It's not so good for reflection.

The clearest way to see your tools clearly is to use something different for a while. When I switched from one notes app to another last year, I noticed things about how I'd been organizing information that I'd never questioned before — because the assumptions were so built-in that I'd stopped seeing them.

> The fish is the last to discover water. The knowledge worker is the last to notice the shape of their tools.

## Designing for Thinking vs. Designing for Output

There's a fundamental tension in tool design between optimizing for output and optimizing for thinking.

Most productivity software optimizes for output. It wants you to produce things: documents, tasks, deliverables, data. The metrics it tracks are completion metrics. The UX is designed around shipping.

This is fine for a lot of work. But for work that is primarily cognitive — where the product is understanding, not a deliverable — this optimization is often counterproductive.

When you're trying to understand something, the enemy is premature closure. The worst thing you can do is decide too soon. The best tools for this kind of work create space for uncertainty, for revision, for holding multiple contradictory ideas at once.

Most productivity tools do the opposite. They want you to commit. To capture. To close the loop.

## What Good Thinking Tools Do

The tools I find most useful for genuine thinking share a few characteristics:

**They're low-friction for capturing.** The fastest path from thought to captured thought should be seconds, not minutes. Every additional step is a thought lost.

**They make connections visible.** Ideas are rarely standalone. The best thinking tools help you see relationships between things — not by enforcing a structure, but by making connections easy to create and easy to discover.

**They don't impose a workflow.** The most useful tools are opinionated about storage and retrieval but flexible about process. They let you develop your own way of working rather than forcing you into theirs.

**They get out of the way.** Eventually, the tool should disappear. The work that requires you to constantly manage the tool is a tax on the work itself.

## A Note on New Tools

Every few years, a new category of thinking tool arrives with significant hype: wikis, then tagging systems, then networked notes, now AI assistants. Each wave brings a genuine innovation and a significant oversell.

The innovation is usually real: these tools do enable kinds of thinking that were harder before. But they don't transform how most people think, because tools can't do that on their own. The transformation requires a genuine change in practice, sustained over time, which most people don't do.

The best thing a new thinking tool can give you isn't a new capability. It's a prompt to examine how you currently think and whether there's a better way.

---

I don't have strong recommendations here — the right tools depend heavily on what you're doing and how you naturally think. But I've come to believe that examining your tools regularly is worth doing, even (especially) when they're working well.

The question isn't just "does this work?" The question is "what is this making me think, and is that what I'd think if I could think about it freely?"

That's a hard question to answer. It's also the right one to ask.
