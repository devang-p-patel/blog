Ira Glass gave an interview once about the gap between taste and ability in creative work. If you haven't read it, the core idea is this: when you start making things, your taste is usually ahead of your ability. You can tell when something is good, but you can't yet make something good. The work you produce falls short of your own standards.

The gap is real. It's also the main reason most people stop.

## The gap doesn't close on its own

You can wait for the gap to close. Some people do. They think that if they just accumulate enough knowledge, read enough, observe enough, eventually the ability will materialize and they'll be ready to start.

This isn't how it works. The gap closes only through output — through actually making things, finishing them, shipping them into the world even when they fall short. There's no shortcut.

The uncomfortable truth is that you have to produce a body of mediocre work before you can produce good work. The mediocre work is not a detour around the good work. It is the path to it.

## Starting versus beginning

There's a distinction I've found useful between starting something and beginning it.

Starting is the act. You open a document, spin up a project, make a first commit. This is the easy part. Beginnings have momentum — the excitement of possibility, the blankness of a constraint-free space.

Beginning is the orientation. It's committing to the idea that this thing will become real, that you'll see it through, that you're willing to produce the bad versions on the way to the good ones.

Most projects get started. Few get properly begun.

## What helps

The thing that has helped me most is shortening the feedback loop. Instead of working for months in private and then releasing something, work in smaller units and get feedback — from yourself, from trusted people, from anyone — sooner.

The feedback doesn't have to be external. The act of finishing something, even something small, teaches you things you can't learn any other way. Each completed thing makes the next one slightly easier.

> The goal isn't to avoid the bad work. The goal is to produce so much work that the bad stuff becomes a small fraction of the total.

---

I've been thinking about this because I'm in the early stages of several things simultaneously — this blog being one of them. The gap is visible to me right now. The words don't yet match what I want to say.

That's fine. It's supposed to be like this at the beginning.
