A year ago, I started sharing my work publicly before it was finished. Before it was good. Sometimes before it made complete sense to me.

The experience has been one of the most useful — and consistently uncomfortable — things I've done in my career.

Here's what I learned.

## The Feedback Loop Changes Everything

When you work in private, you get feedback late. You spend months building in one direction, then show people, then discover you were off. The pivot is expensive. The wasted effort stings.

When you work in public, feedback arrives early and continuously. You post a half-formed idea. Someone tells you it's already been done. Someone else tells you why it won't work. A third person says it's interesting and wants to know more. All of this before you've committed to anything.

This sounds great in theory. In practice, it requires a significant psychological adjustment. You have to become comfortable with looking uncertain, with having wrong opinions in public, with changing your mind visibly.

Most people — most builders, especially — aren't comfortable with this. We've been trained to ship finished things, to not show our work until it's worthy of being shown. Building in public inverts that completely.

## What "Public" Actually Means

There's a spectrum here that people often collapse into a single binary.

At one end: total privacy. Nothing shared until it's done, polished, intentionally released.

At the other end: radical transparency. Every decision, every mistake, every pivot posted in real time.

The useful space is somewhere in the middle, and it shifts depending on what you're building and for whom.

What worked for me: sharing the process, not the product. Writing about what I was thinking and why, rather than showing unfinished code or unpolished interfaces. Observations about the problem space rather than daily changelogs. Questions I was wrestling with rather than answers I hadn't found yet.

This approach created useful conversations without creating a liability. The thinking was public; the implementation details weren't.

## The Unexpected Benefit

The benefit I didn't anticipate: building in public forces you to actually understand what you're doing.

There's a particular kind of fog that descends when you're deep in execution mode. You're busy. You're shipping. But you can't always articulate what problem you're solving, why your approach is the right one, or what you'd do differently if you were starting from scratch.

Writing publicly cuts through that fog. When you're writing for an audience — even a small one — you can't lean on the vague confidence that comes from being inside a project. You have to be specific. You have to make the implicit explicit.

I wrote more clearly about my work in the year I shared it publicly than in the years before, when I was keeping it private. And that clearer writing led to clearer thinking, which led to better decisions.

> Writing is thinking made visible. Publishing is accountability made real.

## The Downsides Are Real

I want to be honest about this: building in public has costs.

**The obvious one** is vulnerability. You're showing unfinished work to people who might judge it. Some will. Most won't, but the fear is persistent.

**The subtler one** is the way public attention can distort what you work on. When you start getting feedback, there's a pull toward whatever generates engagement rather than whatever is most important. The features people comment on aren't always the ones that matter most. The loudest voices online aren't always the most representative of your actual users.

You have to actively resist the optimization for attention. This is harder than it sounds.

**The hardest one** is the time it takes. Writing about your work is work. Finding the right things to share, framing them in useful ways, engaging with the responses — all of this takes time and attention that isn't going into building.

Whether that trade-off is worth it depends on what you're building and what you're optimizing for.

## A Framework for Deciding

Here's how I'd think about it if I were starting again:

**Build in public if**: you're in early exploration, you want to build an audience alongside the product, or you learn better by writing.

**Don't build in public if**: you're operating in a competitive space where information is an asset, you find external feedback destabilizing, or you're past the exploration phase and deep in execution.

**In either case**: write about what you're learning, even if you don't publish it. The clarity you gain is worth it regardless of whether anyone reads it.

---

The most honest thing I can say about building in public is this: it taught me things I couldn't have learned any other way, and I'm still not entirely comfortable with it.

That might be the surest sign that it's worth doing.
